The mathematics of one-solution puzzles in Rikudo³

Greg FitzPatrick · 25 November 2026

A Rikudo puzzle has exactly one solution when its clues leave only one way to walk through every cell once, numbering the cells 1 to N as you go. Rikudo³ plans such puzzles the same way on the cube, the sphere and the pyramid:

  1. Hide a path. Turn the board into a graph and find a random route through every cell.
  2. Add clues. Give every number, and mark some steps of the route as gold chutes.
  3. Prove uniqueness. A solver counts the solutions and stops at two.
  4. Remove clues. Take numbers away one at a time, keeping each removal only if the proof still holds.
  5. Grade it. A second solver that reasons like a person measures how hard the result is.

The shape of the board changes the numbers, not the method. The fewer neighbours a cell has, the fewer clues a puzzle needs and the more the path forces itself, which is why the pyramid plays so differently from the sphere.

The puzzle is a graph

Every board, however it looks, is the same mathematical object: a graph. Each cell is a vertex, and two cells that touch are joined by an edge. The number of edges at a cell is its degree: how many neighbours it has.

A solution is a Hamiltonian path: a route that visits every vertex exactly once, moving only along edges. Writing the cells in path order as v₁, v₂, …, vₙ, the cell vₖ gets the number k, and the rule "each number touches the next" is simply:

Degree is the dial that controls everything else. Walking the path, each step leaves at most d − 1 fresh directions, so a board of N cells allows at most about (d − 1)ᴺ routes. That is a loose ceiling, since most routes get stuck, but it shows the scale: about 10²⁶ on the cube, 10³⁰ on the 100-cell pyramid and 10⁵⁰ on the 72-cell sphere. The clues have to cut that space down to exactly one route.

On this small board, each clue throws away some of the paths that fit, until one is left: the same thing happens on every Rikudo³ board, only with far more paths to rule out.

Three boards, three graphs

The three boards differ in one way that matters most: how many neighbours each cell has.

A graph where every vertex has the same degree d is called d-regular. The cube is 4-regular and the pyramid is 3-regular. The sphere is almost 6-regular, and the exceptions are forced on it by topology.

Why the sphere must have 12 pentagons. Any closed surface shaped like a ball obeys Euler's formula, where V counts corners, E edges and F faces:

Tile a ball with p pentagons and h hexagons, three tiles meeting at every corner. Then F = p + h. Each edge is shared by two tiles, so E = (5p + 6h) / 2, and each corner by three, so V = (5p + 6h) / 3. Substituting, the hexagons cancel out completely:

So every such ball, from a football to the 92-cell board, has exactly 12 pentagons, however many hexagons it has. Goldberg polyhedra place them evenly, which is why the sphere sizes come in steps of 10T + 2: 42, 72, 92.

Why the pyramid has 4m² cells. Cutting each triangular face into m rows gives 1 + 3 + 5 + … + (2m − 1) = m² small triangles, and four faces give 4m²: 36, 64 and 100 for m = 3, 4, 5. A triangle has three sides and each side is shared with exactly one other triangle, even across the pyramid's edges, so every cell has exactly 3 neighbours.

Step 1: hide a path

Every puzzle starts from its answer: a random Hamiltonian path, found before any clue exists. Deciding whether a graph has such a path is NP-complete, one of the classic hard problems with no known fast method for every graph. Our boards are small and regular, though, so a well-steered search finds a path in a fraction of a second.

The generator walks from a random cell and, at each step, chooses among the unvisited neighbours. Three rules keep it out of trouble:

  • Warnsdorff's rule (from the 1823 knight's-tour puzzle): try the neighbour with the fewest onward moves first. Cells that are nearly cut off get visited before they become unreachable. A little randomness breaks ties, so no two paths look alike.
  • Stay connected: the unvisited cells must always form one connected region. If a step splits them in two, the walk could never return for the far part, so that step is abandoned at once.
  • At most one dead end: an unvisited cell with fewer than two unvisited neighbours can only be the path's last cell. Two such cells means the walk is already doomed.

If the walk still gets stuck, it backtracks; after a set number of steps it restarts from a new cell. The finished walk numbers the cells 1 to N, and that numbering is the hidden solution.

Step 2: clues are constraints

A clue is a fact about the hidden path that every candidate path must also satisfy. Rikudo³ uses two kinds:

  • Givens pin a vertex. "Cell c holds 37" removes every route that doesn't pass through c at step 37. Two givens k and k + d also fix the gap between them: the d − 1 cells in between must form a route of exactly d steps.
  • Chutes pin an edge. A gold chute between cells a and b says the path uses that edge, so their numbers differ by exactly 1. A chute doesn't say which number comes first, so it is weaker than a given but cheaper to show.

Two givens are always kept: 1 (the flag) and N (home). Pinning both ends of the path makes the solver's job much smaller, and gives players a place to start from either end. Chutes are drawn from the hidden path's own steps, and no two chutes share a cell, so they never chain into a give-away run.

Think of the space of all possible paths as a haystack. Each clue throws away every path that disagrees with it. The puzzle is ready when exactly one path is left, and that has to be proven, not hoped for.

Step 3: prove there is only one

Uniqueness is proven by counting: a solver searches every path that fits the clues and stops as soon as it finds a second one. A count of 1 is a proof that the hidden path is the only answer; a count of 2 means the clues are too weak.

The search starts at the cell holding 1 and extends the path one number at a time, backing up when it hits a wall. Searched naively, that tree would be astronomically large. Four tests cut whole branches off before they grow:

  1. Connectivity. The unvisited cells must still form one connected region; otherwise some of them can never be reached.
  2. Distance to the next given. If the path is at number k on cell u, and the next given is g on cell t, then t must be reachable in time, counting only free cells:
  3. Dead ends. A free cell with fewer than two usable neighbours can only be the path's end, and the end is already known (home). Any other such cell kills the branch.
  4. Chutes force the next step. If the current cell has a chute that hasn't been used yet, the path must take it next.

With these tests a proof takes from a few hundred to a few hundred thousand search steps. When generating, the solver gets a step budget, and running out counts as "not proven", so the clue stays. That makes the generator cautious but never wrong. Every puzzle in the published packs was checked again afterwards with no budget at all.

Step 4: take clues away

The generator starts with every number given, which is trivially unique, then tries removing them one at a time in random order. A removal stays only if step 3 still proves a single solution. Below Expert it is also undone if the puzzle would grade harder than the level asked for. When no single given can be removed any more, the puzzle is minimal.

Minimal is not the same as minimum. A different removal order can end with fewer clues, and finding the true minimum means checking every subset of clues, which is far out of reach. Sudoku shows how hard that is: proving that no Sudoku with 16 clues has a unique solution took an exhaustive computer search, finished in 2012.

The trend runs against intuition: boards with more neighbours need more clues, not fewer. Every extra side is another way for the path to go, so more of it must be pinned down. At 3 neighbours the board's structure does most of the work. One 36-cell Expert pyramid in the test needed just 2 givens, the flag and home, plus a few chutes.

Step 5: measure the difficulty

A unique puzzle can still be trivial, so every puzzle is graded by a second solver that never guesses blindly. It solves the puzzle using only moves a person can make, always trying the simplest kind first, and scores each placed number by the technique it needed:

Range reasoning is the distance test from step 3, used the way a person uses it:

The rating is the sum of points over every step, so bigger boards and harder techniques both raise it. The level comes from the average points per step, h = rating ÷ steps: Easy under 2, Medium under 2.8, Hard under 3.8, Expert above. Needing trial and error or deep search often also raises the level, whatever the average. Below Easy sits Beginner, defined by technique rather than average: every step is next in line or a chute, apart from at most a handful of link-logic steps (2, or 4% of the cells on big boards), with no range reasoning at all. Every Beginner puzzle can be solved with only the moves the tutorial teaches. The generator then aims for the requested level and labels every puzzle with its measured one.

Why the pyramid solves itself. Link logic is a counting argument about degree. A cell in the middle of the path uses exactly 2 of its sides. On the pyramid a cell has only 3 sides, so ruling out a single side forces the other two, and each forced link rules out sides on the next cells. One deduction sets off a chain. On the sphere a cell has 6 sides, and ruling one out still leaves 5 candidates for 2 links, so almost nothing follows. That is why the same five techniques give the pyramid long runs of easy steps between hard ones, and the sphere long stretches where only range reasoning helps.

On the triangle, one ruled-out side settles the cell completely. On the square and the hexagon it settles nothing, so the solver has to fall back on harder techniques.
